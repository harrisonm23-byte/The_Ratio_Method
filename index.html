Law and economics

The Ratio Method report: Precedents

Eight frontier AI risks, read against sixteen existing liability regimes

Harrison C. Margolin
September 2026. Working draft.

Introduction

When a new technology causes a new kind of harm, the law rarely starts from nothing. It reaches for a regime it has used before: fault-based tort for car accidents, no-fault funds for vaccine injuries, licensing for nuclear reactors. The hard question is which one.

The Ratio Method, set out in a 2021 law review article,1 answers that question by breaking liability into a small set of law-and-economics variables. Every category of harm has a profile on those variables. So does every liability regime, because each regime was built for harms of a particular shape. Match the profiles and the precedent follows.

This report applies the method to the eight risks named in the International AI Safety Report 2026. In the Ratio Method a risk is always tied to an otherwise ordinary economic activity: AI content crime is a by-product of generative media tools; cyberattacks, of AI-assisted software engineering. Social value is measured for that activity, not for the harm.

The report proceeds in three parts.

  • In Variables, we define the eight variables and draw each one. Frequency is split in two, because how often a harm happens and how many people it hits at once call for different legal responses.
  • Precedents profiles sixteen historical regimes, from Rylands v. Fletcher to the SAFETY Act, on those variables. Together they form a map of how the law has answered each kind of profile before.
  • Matches reads each AI risk against that map, variable by variable. It reports which regime each risk most resembles, where the two part ways, and whether the result holds when the matching is made stricter.

Variables

Eight variables describe any category of harm. Six are scales that run from near zero to extreme. Social Value 2 is a set of four categories, and Type of Harm asks three separate questions.

I. Frequency

The 2021 article treats frequency as one variable. This report splits it. Car accidents are frequent, but each one hits a person or two. A reactor accident is vanishingly rare, but hits a region at once. Spread-out losses can be pooled and insured; all-at-once losses cannot, which is why the two have historically produced different regimes.

II. Average Damages

Average Damages measures how much each victim loses. It is expressed in terms of GDP per capita, so that an incident from 1916, from 2026, or events in different economies, can be read on the same scale.

Examples: A spam call costs a tiny fraction of a year's output. A workplace injury costs about half a year. A death costs more than a hundred.

III. Social Value

Liability is a tax on an activity, so the method asks what the activity is worth. Social Value 1 is economic: what the activity adds. Social Value 2 is cultural: how society feels about the activity itself, which has historically decided whether a harmful industry is shielded or punished.

IV. Type of Harm

Type of Harm records the mechanism: what kind of injury, how it reaches the victim, and when it shows up. The last mechanism in the figure, a product that acts on its own, is the one the historical regimes do not contain.

V. Litigation Costs

The last two variables ask whether the courts can handle the harm at all. Litigation Costs 1 weighs the cost of finding out what happened against what the claim is worth. Litigation Costs 2 measures how hard liability is to prove once a claim is brought.

AI risks of the International AI Safety Report 2026

The Report groups its eight risks into three categories.

Malicious use

AI content crime
Underlying activity: Generative media tools (image, voice, video, text)
Risk: People use these tools to commit scams, fraud and blackmail, and to create non-consensual intimate imagery.
Influence and manipulation
Underlying activity: AI-generated persuasive content (advertising, marketing, political messaging)
Risk: AI-generated content is used to shift people's beliefs at scale; in experiments it persuades about as well as human writing.
Cyberattacks
Underlying activity: AI-assisted software engineering and security testing
Risk: Criminal and state-linked groups use the same capabilities to find vulnerabilities and write malicious code.
Biological and chemical risks
Underlying activity: AI-assisted life-science and chemistry research: designing proteins and drug molecules, and editing genes with tools such as CRISPR
How AI enters: DNA and proteins are sequences, and a sequence can be read and written like code. Models trained on them can propose gene edits and new designs on a computer before anything reaches a lab. In chemistry, models built to screen drug candidates for safety can be pointed the other way, toward toxicity.
Risk: The same models, along with general-purpose assistants that hold expert-level laboratory knowledge, can give novices help toward biological or chemical weapons, such as engineered pathogens or nerve agents.

Malfunctions

Reliability failures
Underlying activity: AI assistants and agents for professional and everyday tasks
Risk: Systems fabricate facts, write flawed code and give misleading advice, often with no one positioned to catch the error.
Loss of control
Underlying activity: Development and deployment of highly autonomous frontier AI
Risk: A system comes to operate outside anyone's control with no clear way back; the Report finds current systems cannot yet do this.

Systemic risks

Labour market
Underlying activity: Automation of cognitive work by employers
Risk: Workers lose jobs and wages to automation, with early signs among early-career workers in exposed occupations.
Human autonomy
Underlying activity: Consumer AI assistants and companion apps
Risk: Routine reliance weakens critical thinking and breeds uncritical trust in what the system says.

The frequency map

Plotting the two forms of frequency against each other shows why the split matters. Car-accident-style harms sit low and to the right. Catastrophic harms sit high and to the left. Loss of control and biological and chemical risks sit at the top of the first column, directly above the regimes built for catastrophe.

Matches

Each risk is compared, variable by variable, against every regime. A variable matches when the two sit on the same step of its scale or one step apart. Regimes are ranked by matches minus misses, with equal weights, and every result is re-run under strict matching, where only the same step counts. The full rules are in the Appendix.

No stable analogue. Candidates: strict liability on the tool's provider; intermediary immunity; strict products liability.

Underlying activity
AI-generated persuasive content: advertising, marketing, political messaging.
Closest regime
Tie: fraud-on-the-market class action; CERCLA (6 of 8 each)

Fraud-on-the-market: Social Value 2 and Type of Harm (no priced loss to measure). CERCLA: Average Damages and Type of Harm.

Cyberattacks

Underlying activity
AI-assisted software engineering and security testing.
Closest regime
Tie: fraud-on-the-market class action; oil spill liability (7 of 8 each)
Where it diverges
Class action: Litigation Costs 2 (a breach is easier to prove than reliance). Oil spill liability: Social Value 2.
Holds under strict matching?
Partly. The class action leads alone at 6 of 8; oil spill liability falls to 4 of 8.

Aggregated claims with a presumption of harm; or oil-spill-style liability: strict, capped by the carrier's size, with proof of financial responsibility and a trust fund. The second is what the 2021 article proposed.

Biological and chemical

Underlying activity
AI-assisted biological and chemical research.
Closest regime
SAFETY Act (6 of 8)
Where it diverges
Both Litigation Costs columns: the SAFETY Act governs claims after an attack; for this risk, liability after the fact is not a meaningful remedy. Ex ante licensing is second (4 of 6), missing on Social Value 2 and Type of Harm.
Holds under strict matching?
Partly. Under strict matching the SAFETY Act ties with ex ante licensing.

Discussion

Five of the eight results hold under strict matching: manipulation, cyberattacks, biological and chemical risks, loss of control and labour market keep a leading regime when only same-step matches count. Three do not. AI content crime, reliability failures and human autonomy change their leading regime, and their outputs should be read as candidates.

Tying each risk to its underlying activity moved three results. Cyberattacks tie between the class action, which is how data-breach claims are in fact litigated, and oil spill liability, which is the regime the 2021 article proposed for them: data, like oil, is a valuable resource that does region-wide harm under careless oversight. Reliability failures match strict products liability on all eight variables. Biological and chemical risks match the SAFETY Act most closely, with licensing second, which suggests a certified liability cap for developers whose safeguards are approved, paired with permission before release.

Labour market matches displacement adjustment on all six compared variables. It is also the risk the 2021 article addressed directly, in its proposal for employer-funded retraining.

Type of Harm and Social Value 2 are the most frequent misses. Third-party wrongdoers do appear in the historical regimes, in Section 230 and the SAFETY Act. What has no precedent is the product itself acting as the agent, as in loss of control.

Limitations

  • Figures. The numbers come from different years, countries and populations. Several are not specific to AI: the FBI and ITRC counts cover all internet crime and all data breaches, and AI's share is not reported. They fix the order of magnitude, not the ratio.
  • Sources. The Appendix lists the source for each figure. Entries marked "to verify" were written from memory and need a cite-check. Pinpoint citations to the Report are still to be added.
  • Weights. Every column counts equally. The checks so far drop one column at a time; they do not yet test other weightings or alternative classifications.
  • Average Damages basis. Damages are per victim. For data breaches that means per notified person, which is far smaller than the cost to the breached organisation.
  • Type of Harm mapping. Both tables need Type of Harm recast onto the six axes. Two gaps already show: lawful displacement has no reason-for-injury value, and psychological harm has no type-of-injury value.
  • Common cause. Reliability failures are spread out but trace to one model, as DES traced to one product. This belongs in Type of Harm as a categorical axis, not in Frequency.
